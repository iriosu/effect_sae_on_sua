# Independent referee assessment: QJE readiness (issue #13)

*Basis: the 2026-07-17 build of `documents/draft/main_AM.pdf` (39 pp., including the colored edit markers), the section sources, and the appendix. Per the issue, the paper is judged as its **intended finished version** — I credit the robustness the draft itself announces as planned (wild cluster bootstrap, unconditional 9th-grade-entrant sample) and ignore draft hygiene that clearly reflects work in progress, except where it changes substance. Part 1 is the referee report; Part 2 is the ≤2-week agent-doable plan mapped to open issues.*

## Part 1 — Referee report

### Summary of the paper

The paper estimates the effect of Chile's transition from decentralized, school-run admissions to a nationwide centralized school-choice clearinghouse (SAE, deferred acceptance, rolled out region by region 2016–2019) on downstream college-admissions outcomes in the country's separate centralized college-admissions system (SUA). The design is a staggered difference-in-differences (Callaway–Sant'Anna, doubly robust) on class-level averages for 25,855 graduating classes at 2,888 schools (1.07M students), with not-yet-treated public/voucher schools and 395 never-treated private schools as comparisons, a transition band of partially exposed classes set aside via the anticipation parameter, and a fixed pre-treatment class within each school as the reference. Headline results: applying to the centralized college system rises 4.5pp (+10.4% of the 0.432 pre-mean), admission 5.1pp (+15.4%), enrollment 4.1pp (+15.5%); high-school GPA is flat; the composite Language-and-Math (LM) percentile measure falls 1.6pp. Gains are concentrated among female students (+7.8/+7.5/+6.4pp), high-vulnerability schools, and low-value-added schools, and load onto less selective, under-subscribed programs, with a significant −3.0pp effect on admission to the top-10% most selective programs. Mechanism evidence: segregation by academic ability falls at the district level; truthful reporting of the top-ranked option rises 9.3pp; application lists get shorter but more valid (+13.4pp valid-application share).

This is a genuinely good question with a unique setting and excellent data. The full-pipeline administrative linkage and the "two sequential centralized markets" framing are assets no competing paper has. My assessment: the paper is not yet at the QJE bar, for reasons that are mostly fixable, and several of the needed fixes are already flagged in the draft or the issue tracker. The binding constraints are (i) the outcome set stops at *within-system* participation, so the headline "expands access to higher education" is not yet established; (ii) the headline sample conditions on post-treatment behavior; (iii) inference is internally inconsistent and not yet defensible with 16 regions; (iv) the treatment is a bundle whose non-SAE components are untested; and (v) the mechanism section is suggestive and contains internal contradictions the draft itself would be refereed on.

### 1. Contribution and positioning

**Is the question first-order?** Yes. Centralized assignment is now the default policy technology in education markets (the paper's own Neilson figure: ~40% of countries), and whether K-12 centralization propagates into college access is unstudied. The equity heterogeneity speaks directly to the design debates in Terrier–Pathak–Ren and Campos–Kearns.

**Is the answer novel relative to the frontier?** The *margin* is novel. Abdulkadiroğlu–Agarwal–Pathak (2017 AER) quantify within-market welfare gains from coordinating one market; Campos–Kearns (2024 QJE) find college-enrollment gains from a district-level choice expansion via competition; Terrier–Pathak–Ren (AEJ: Applied, published version) find the England IA→DA transition *reduced* low-SES access to good schools; Kutscher–Nath–Urzúa (2023) and Botbol–Gazmuri study the same Chilean reform but stop at school-level sorting/segregation outcomes. Nobody has traced a national centralization into a second, downstream centralized market. That cross-market question — do institutions students pass through change how they behave in the next mechanism they meet — is the paper's best card and is currently underplayed. The Chile-vs-England sign reversal (inclusion here, crowding-out there) is a second underplayed card: the draft cites Terrier et al. but never explains *why* the signs differ (plausibly: Chile removed schools' selective screening and had slack capacity in the destination market; England removed strategic self-selection by high-SES parents in a congested market). A QJE reader will want that reconciliation in the introduction.

**Does the marginal contribution clear the QJE bar as the paper stands?** Not yet, for one substantive reason: every outcome is participation *in the SUA system*. A skeptical referee's one-line summary is "students exposed to a centralized platform in secondary school later use a centralized platform for college — a mechanical familiarity effect on channel choice." What makes the result first-order is whether the marginal enrollee is *new to higher education* (net access gain) or *diverted* from off-platform options — non-SUA universities, IPs, CFTs (cf. Kapor–Karnani–Neilson 2024 on off-platform options in this exact market). Section 3.3 states the data cover enrollment in *any* higher-education institution, so the total-enrollment margin is constructible, yet no table reports it. Establishing the net margin (plus destination quality) is the single highest-value addition to the paper; it converts a channel-substitution curiosity into an access result. Until then, the abstract's "meaningfully expand access to higher education" and the conclusion's equity claims overreach what is shown.

**Positioning gaps.** The biggest omission in the references is Abdulkadiroğlu–Agarwal–Pathak (2017 AER) itself (issue #5 already flags it); Terrier et al. should be cited as published; Campos–Kearns is out as QJE 2024 (bib says "Forthcoming"); Akbarpour et al. (2022 JPubE, unequal outside options in Chile) belongs in the outside-options discussion, especially if the net-enrollment margin is added. The lit review is a separate section written as three strands plus a contribution subsection; for QJE it should compress into the introduction (the standalone rewrite in `lit_review_standalone/` exists but is unmerged — issue #5).

### 2. Identification

The design has real strengths a referee will credit: the transition-band construction (setting aside partially exposed classes via the anticipation parameter, with the clean reference class four years pre-treatment) is careful and unusually transparent (Appendix 7.4–7.5 is exemplary); treatment dates are verified against SAE administrative records; the private-school placebo is the right falsification and it passes (Table 8, all coefficients insignificant); pre-treatment leads are clean with an exact-RI joint test (p = 0.46–0.84, robust to excluding the one significant deep lead). Five concerns, in descending order of severity:

**(a) The headline sample conditions on post-treatment behavior.** The estimation sample keeps single-school, on-time completers — school-switching, dropout, and retention are all outcomes the reform can move, and the paper's own segregation results (Table 9) show the reform re-sorted students across schools. The draft knows this: the blue-marked Sample paragraph reports robustness to relaxing the filter, and Appendix 7.4 calls the unconditional all-entrants sample "a planned robustness check." For QJE this is backwards: the unconditional 9th-grade-entrant class ITT should be the *headline* (it is also the policy-relevant object), with the single-school sample as the mechanism-friendly secondary cut. This is issue #8 item 1 and it is rightly Priority 1.

**(b) The post-treatment counterfactual rests entirely on 395 private schools through the COVID and exam-reform window.** Appendix 7.5 states it plainly: once the rollout completes, no public school remains four-plus years from its own treatment date, so *every* post-treatment contrast is against private fee-paying schools. Post-treatment admission outcomes are measured in 2021–2025 — entirely inside the COVID disruption and the PSU→PDT→PAES transition (no post-treatment admission year falls under the pre-reform PSU regime), on top of the gratuidad era. The second difference removes these shocks only if they hit private and public/voucher schools equally, which for COVID (connectivity, closure duration) is doubtful and for exam redesign (content changes have SES gradients) untested. The private-school placebo mitigates but does not resolve this, because it tests region-adoption timing, not differential aggregate-shock incidence. Needed: drop-2020/21-cohort robustness, wave-by-wave event studies, and an explicit defense of private schools as the counterfactual carrier — plus the honest statement of this design fact in the main text (it currently lives only in the appendix; a referee who finds it there will feel it was buried).

**(c) The bundled-treatment threat (issue #3) is unaddressed.** The 2015 School Inclusion Law bundles copayment elimination, a selection ban, SEP-linked resources, and centralized assignment. The rollout dates identify SAE, but schools may adjust the other margins *when SAE arrives locally* (gratuity conversion, copayment cuts, vulnerable-student intake). If so, the estimand is "the SAE-centered reform bundle." The clean fix is the one the external comment prescribed: school-year event studies for copayment, gratuity status, SEP receipt, enrollment, and vulnerability share around first SAE exposure, stratified by private-subsidized/high-copayment schools. Either the margins are flat (the mechanism claim survives) or they move (reframe as "transparency-and-access reform," which the intro's own language half-adopts already). Right now the paper asserts the narrow interpretation without the test.

**(d) "Pseudo-random assignment" is asserted, never documented.** The introduction says regions were selected pseudo-randomly; the conclusion says "partially randomized"; the empirical section says timing was unannounced. No citation, document, or balance evidence supports any of these load-bearing claims. Magallanes (pilot) and Metropolitana (last, and the source of the only significant deep pre-trend lead at e = −8) were clearly non-random; the credible variation is the 2017-vs-2018 split among 14 regions. The paper needs: the ministry documentation of how the split was made (needs author knowledge — issue #4's first task), a wave-balance table on pre-period region observables, and prose that states precisely which comparisons the randomization claim covers.

**(e) Market-level estimand and interference.** Treatment is at the region level but the college market is national: treated-region applicants compete with not-yet-treated-region applicants for the same seats, so displacement (which Table 6 documents at the top decile, −3.0pp) mechanically contaminates not-yet-treated comparisons upward. The paper's slack-capacity argument (nearly half of programs failed to fill their seats in 2025, per footnote 3) is the right response, but SUTVA across regions is never discussed. Issue #1's request — state the commune/region-level ITT estimand explicitly, note it sidesteps household self-selection, and audit units — remains open. Related: the ATT aggregation weights by classes, not students; small rural classes count as much as large urban ones. Student-weighted aggregates should be reported (they are the policy object), and the "control for cohort size" phrase in the Table 1 note should be stated precisely.

### 3. Mechanisms

The Section 5 framework (preferences / beliefs / strategic behavior, via Larroucau et al. 2024) is sensible, and the application-quality results (Table 11, Figure 3: shorter, more-valid lists; +13.4pp valid share; fewer 5+ lists) are the most convincing mechanism evidence — they tie cleanly to the admission gains. But the section falls short of the bar in four ways:

1. **It tests treatment effects on intermediate outcomes, not a decomposition.** The sorting/school-quality/match decomposition of issue #2 (heterogeneous VAM with time-varying school effects, components summing to the total) is what would let the paper say *how much* of the enrollment gain flows through re-sorting versus behavior. The segregation table proves re-sorting happened, which cuts both ways: it is a mechanism, and it is a compositional threat to the class-level design (see 2a). Without the decomposition, the paper cannot separate "students behave differently" from "different students sit in the classes."
2. **The LM outcome conflates margins.** LM assigns non-takers a zero, so the −1.6pp effect mixes exam-taking (which the reform should *raise* — more applicants must sit the exam) with conditional performance (which mean-reverts if marginal takers are weaker). The obvious decomposition — P(take exam) and score-conditional-on-taking, plus the ranking-score sanity outcome (issues #8 items 2/4, #10) — is announced ("Section 5 discusses") but the discussion never delivers it. As written, a referee cannot tell whether the negative LM effect is composition (benign, even confirmatory) or real achievement decline (which would undercut the aspirations story).
3. **Table 10 is not yet a credible exhibit.** It conditions on applicants (the treatment moves who applies — selection into the sample, a classic bad-control problem), uses an unexplained specification with "Constant" rows unlike every other table, has three sample sizes (7,168 / 2,019 / 7,241) with no explanation, no pre-treatment means, and no definitions of "Bias" or "Adm. Probability" outcomes in the text. The +9.3pp truthful-top-reporting result is interesting and worth keeping, but it needs the CS-DiD treatment, defined outcomes, and a bounding argument for the selection-into-application problem.
4. **The gender result — a headline finding — has no mechanism analysis in the paper.** Female effects are 3–4× male effects, yet the draft's entire treatment is one purple footnote citing Carlana et al. (2026). The PACE robustness work (issue #11) is done and decisive (female effects survive stripping PACE-track applications and dropping ever-PACE schools; the PACE-exposure DiD is timing-orthogonal), and the mechanisms battery (issue #14) is running. None of it is in the draft yet. For QJE, the female concentration needs either an explained channel (rank-based admission weights favoring girls' GPAs; application-friction relief; field-of-study supply) or a properly cited residual-behavioral interpretation anchored on the PACE evidence.

Also: Section 5.1's peer-quality story would be strengthened by noting the tension it creates with the Kutscher et al. stability claim used in Section 4.1.2 — SES composition stable but academic composition not; the draft uses each fact where convenient without connecting them.

### 4. Robustness and inference

**The inference story is currently self-contradictory and would be caught immediately.** Page 11 (blue edit): "We cluster standard errors at the region level, since whole regions enter the system at once" — the right principle. Appendix 7.5: standard errors come from a multiplier bootstrap "clustered at the school level," with region-level wild cluster bootstrap "a planned robustness check." The results text says "double robust standard errors [clustered at the region level]" (also: "doubly robust" describes the estimator, not the SEs). These cannot all be true. With treatment assigned at the region level, school-level clustering understates uncertainty; with 16 regions (and one pilot + one mega-region), conventional cluster-robust inference over-rejects. The paper already has the right tool built for pre-trends — the exact RI over the 1,001 admissible 2017/2018 assignments — and should extend it to the main ATTs, reporting RI p-values alongside wild-cluster-bootstrap and the current bootstrap for every headline estimate (issues #4, #7). Until then, every star in the paper is provisional. (Internal note: the gender-table SEs also moved materially across `did`-package versions in replication — e.g., male assigned 0.006 vs 0.017 — so all tables should be regenerated under one pinned version before resubmission of any kind.)

**What exists is good**: clean-lead event study with RI joint test; private-school placebo; timing-shift placebo (Figure 2); TWFE comparison consistent with CS estimates; sample-filter robustness noted; VA-measure robustness (multi-cohort, corr > 0.80). **What is missing**: RI/wild bootstrap on the main effects; unconditional-sample headline; COVID/exam-reform-window cuts; leave-one-region-out (Magallanes drives e = 4; Metropolitana drives e = −8); student-weighted aggregation; and a summary-statistics/balance table — beyond the filter counts of Appendix 7.4, the paper has no descriptive table of the sample at all.

**Internal contradictions a referee would list verbatim** (these also feed issue #12):

- **Displacement**: Abstract and intro: "some displacement in highly selective programs." Conclusion: "no evidence of displacement in highly selective programs… without generating congestion." Table 6 col. 4: −0.030*** on the top decile; Table 13: −0.035*** on Q5. The conclusion is wrong as written; the finding is real and actually interesting — own it and connect it to Terrier et al.'s congestion mechanism.
- **LM in the mechanisms section**: Section 5.2 claims "no detectable effects on GPA or on the average score between the Math and Verbal components," but Table 1 reports LM −0.016** at 5%. The 5.2 argument ("preparation channel not at play") currently rests on a misstatement of the paper's own headline table.
- **Applied ≠ Applied**: Table 1 "Applied" is +0.045 (pre-mean 0.432); Table 11 "≥1 program" is +0.054 (pre-mean 0.426), same N = 28,483. These should be the same outcome; if the definitions differ (e.g., valid applications only), the text must say so; if not, one table is stale.
- **Transition-band width**: the main text and Table 7 set aside *two* transition classes (band = event times −1, −2; reference −3), while Appendix 7.4 and Table 16 define the band as classes formed *one to three* years before treatment (6,707 classes), say the Callaway–Sant'Anna anticipation parameter is "set to three," and place the reference at −4. Both stories are internally coherent (classroom-overlap vs. school-overlap counting), but the paper asserts both; which one the estimation actually uses must be pinned down and stated once.
- **Rollout window**: intro says 2016–2020; background, Figure 1, and Appendix 7.4 say 2016–2019. If the intro counts entry years and the rest admission-process years, define the convention once and use it everywhere.

### 5. Writing, framing, exhibits

The prose at the paragraph level is clean and the appendix is genuinely excellent. The top-of-paper packaging is where the QJE standard bites:

- **No numbers up front.** The abstract (~200 words) and the entire introduction contain not a single magnitude. QJE-style: pre-means, percentage effects, and the female/vulnerability concentration belong in abstract sentence 3 and intro paragraphs 4–6. "Robust across specifications" should not be one of the "four main results."
- **Title** lacks the setting: "…Evidence from Chile's Staggered Centralization" (or similar) is stronger and standard.
- **Intro structure**: results arrive qualitatively in one paragraph; the literature is a separate 4-page section; the roadmap skips Section 5 entirely. Compress the lit review into the intro (the standalone rewrite exists), state the estimand (market-level ITT, issue #1) in the intro, and add the England-reconciliation paragraph.
- **Exhibits**: Figure 2's caption is literally "Caption"; Figure 1 is cross-referenced as "Figure 3.4"; Table 1 is titled "Regression Results" (say what it shows); Table 10's outcomes are undefined; no table reports controls/estimator/clustering in a consistent note format; no summary-statistics table exists. Bib: duplicate entries (Cullen 2006a/b; Deming 2014a/b; Rios et al. 2021a/b), several venue-less entries (Larroucau–Rios 2025; Rios–Sharma 2026; Borusyak–Jaravel), stale statuses (Terrier et al.; Campos–Kearns), and the intro's first citation renders as "…secondary schools Neilson (2024)" (a `\citet`/`\citep` slip). Issue #5 covers most of this.

### Verdict

**As it stands (judged as the intended finished version): not yet at the QJE bar — at QJE this draft would draw a reject-and-resubmit-elsewhere or a very demanding major revision, with the referee's enthusiasm depending almost entirely on whether the net-enrollment margin turns out positive.** The same paper is close to a strong R&R at AEJ: Applied or JPubE today. The question, setting, and data are QJE-grade; the current outcome set, inference, and mechanism depth are not yet.

The five things that most move the needle toward a QJE-viable submission:

1. **Total higher-education enrollment and destination quality** (new outcome family; the access-vs-substitution question). Everything else is second-order to this.
2. **Unconditional 9th-grade-entrant ITT as the headline sample** (#8 item 1), with the market-level estimand stated (#1).
3. **Design-based inference overhaul**: RI + wild cluster bootstrap for all headline effects, one consistent clustering story, tables regenerated under a pinned estimator version (#4, #7).
4. **Bundle event studies + randomization documentation** (#3, #4): show the other SIL margins don't move at SAE entry (or reframe), and document the 2017/2018 assignment with a wave-balance table.
5. **Mechanism upgrade**: LM decomposition (taking vs. conditional score), gender mechanisms + PACE robustness into the paper (#11, #14), Table 10 rebuilt, and — for the full QJE version — the sorting/quality/match decomposition (#2).
